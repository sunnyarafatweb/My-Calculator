What makes a calculator scientific

The dividing line is not the number of keys. It is whether the machine waits. A four-function calculator acts the moment you press an operator: type 2 + 3 × 4 and it adds first because that is what you asked for first, giving 20. A scientific calculator holds the whole expression, reads it as a piece of mathematics, and then answers — giving 14, because multiplication outranks addition no matter what order your thumbs went in.

Everything else follows from that one design choice. Once the machine is reading an expression rather than a sequence of button presses, brackets become meaningful, functions can take arguments, and a line like sin(30) + √16 is a single question with a single answer rather than four separate steps you have to stitch together yourself.

The rest of the keys are shorthand for operations that turn up constantly in physics, chemistry, engineering and any maths course past algebra: trigonometric ratios, the two logarithms, powers and roots in both directions, factorials, and a way to write numbers with twenty zeros in them without pressing zero twenty times.

Why 2 + 3 × 4 is 14 and not 20

The rule is old and universal, and the acronym you learned for it depends on where you went to school — PEMDAS in the United States, BODMAS or BIDMAS across much of the Commonwealth. They describe the same ranking:

  • Brackets first, innermost outward.
  • Then powers and roots.
  • Then multiplication and division, worked left to right as equals.
  • Then addition and subtraction, also left to right.

The pair that trips people is multiplication and division. They do not rank above one another; they share a rank and are settled left to right. So 8 / 2 × 4 is 16, not 1 — the division happens first because it comes first, not because division outranks multiplication.

If you want a different order, say so with brackets. (2 + 3) × 4 gives 20. Brackets are not a formality here; they are the only way to override the ranking, and they cost two key presses.

This calculator also fills in multiplication signs you leave out, the way a textbook does. Press 2 then π and it reads 2 × π. Press 3 then ( and it reads 3 × (. That is convenient, and it is also worth knowing about, because it means a stray digit next to a bracket quietly becomes a multiplication rather than an error.

Degrees or radians, and why it matters

An angle is a rotation, and a rotation can be counted in more than one unit. A full turn is 360 degrees, or 2π radians — roughly 6.283. Both describe the same trip around the circle. The number 30 therefore means completely different rotations depending on which unit you meant: a modest thirty-degree wedge, or thirty radians, which is nearly five full revolutions.

So the switch at the top of this calculator is not a preference. It changes the answer:

  • sin 30 in DEG is exactly 0.5.
  • sin 30 in RAD is -0.988031624093.

Neither is wrong. They are answers to different questions. As a rough guide, degrees are what geometry, surveying and most school trigonometry problems use, while radians are what calculus, physics and anything involving angular velocity assume. If a trig answer comes out somewhere you did not expect, check this switch before you check your arithmetic — it is the single most common reason a correct method produces a wrong number.

The inverse keys follow the same setting in reverse. sin-1 0.5 returns 30 in DEG mode and about 0.5236 in RAD mode, which is π/6 written as a decimal.

Powers, roots and scientific notation

Powers come in three flavours on this keypad, and the difference is where the number goes.

  • x2 and x3 go after the number, like a suffix. Press 7 then x2 for 49.
  • xy sits between two numbers. Press 2, then xy, then 10 for 1024.
  • ex and 10x go before a number, because the base is already fixed. Press 10x then 3 for 1000.

Roots mirror this. The plain √ and 3√ keys open a bracket in front of whatever you type next. The y√x key sits between two numbers and takes the root first: press 3, then y√x, then 8 to get 2, because 2 cubed is 8.

Then there is EXP, which exists because scientific work is full of numbers that are mostly zeros. Avogadro's constant is 602,214,076,000,000,000,000,000. Written in scientific notation it is 6.02214076 × 1023, and on this keypad it is 6.02214076, EXP, 23. The EXP key means “times ten to the power of” in one press. For small numbers, follow EXP with the ± key: 1.6 EXP ± 19 gives 1.6 × 10-19, the elementary charge.

Answers come back in the same notation once they get unwieldy. Anything needing more than twelve digits before the decimal point, or smaller than about a hundred-billionth, is shown as a coefficient times a power of ten rather than as a wall of zeros.

Three ways to reuse a number

Multi-step problems always reach a point where you need a figure you already worked out. There are three separate mechanisms here, and they are good at different things.

Ans

The most immediate. Ans drops the last answer straight into the current expression, so you can chain steps without writing anything down. Work out a subtotal, press an operator, press Ans, and carry on. It always holds the most recent result and nothing older.

Memory

M+ adds what is on screen to a stored running total; M− subtracts from it. MR pastes that total back. It is a small accumulator rather than a clipboard, which makes it well suited to summing a column of results that each need their own calculation first. A green M appears beside the mode switch whenever the store is holding something, and once you have recalled, the MR key offers MC for one press so you can wipe it.

History

Every completed calculation is listed beside the keypad, most recent at the top, with the expression above the result. Tap any line and it goes back into the calculator so you can adjust one number and run it again. This is the one to use when you are checking homework and need to see what you actually typed three steps ago, rather than only what it came to. The list lives in the page and clears when you leave.

Mistakes this calculator will not save you from

A calculator answers the question you typed, which is not always the question you meant. These are the ones worth knowing about:

  • The wrong angle unit. Covered above, and worth repeating because it produces confident, plausible, wrong numbers rather than errors.
  • log where you wanted ln. log is base 10 and ln is base e. Both are valid; only one is what your chemistry sheet asked for.
  • Rounding partway through. Copying a mid-step answer to two decimals and typing it back in throws away precision that the later steps then multiply. Ans, memory and history all exist so you never have to.
  • A minus that meant two different things. -32 is negative nine, because the squaring binds tighter than the sign. (-3)2 is positive nine. If you meant the second, the brackets are not optional.
  • An implied multiplication you did not intend. Because 2(3) reads as six, a mistyped digit next to a bracket produces an answer rather than a complaint.

What this calculator does not do

Being straight about the edges is more useful than pretending there are none.

  • It works to roughly twelve significant digits, which is the practical limit of standard double-precision arithmetic. It is not an arbitrary-precision tool; for very long integers use the big number calculator.
  • It evaluates, it does not solve. There is no algebra, no unknowns and no symbolic simplification — you will get a decimal, not π/6.
  • It does not graph. For plotting a function against its inputs you need a graphing tool.
  • There are no hyperbolic functions, no permutations or combinations, no statistics mode, and no complex numbers. For spread and averages, use the standard deviation calculator or the mean, median, mode and range calculator.
  • Factorials are exact for whole numbers up to 170 and fall back to the gamma function for decimals. Above 170 the true value exceeds what the number format can hold, and you will see an error rather than a wrong figure.
  • Nothing is saved between visits. The history is cleared when the page closes.

For a single operation with its method spelled out step by step, the dedicated pages are often the better tool: exponents, roots, logarithms, scientific notation, and the quadratic formula. Working in another base entirely is handled by the binary and hex calculators.

Frequently asked questions

Why does this calculator give 14 for 2 + 3 × 4?

Because multiplication is resolved before addition. The calculator reads the whole expression before answering, so 3 × 4 is settled first and 2 is added to the 12. A basic four-function calculator answers each key press in turn and returns 20 instead. If you actually want the addition first, wrap it: (2 + 3) × 4.

What is the difference between DEG and RAD mode?

They are two units for the same angle. A full turn is 360 degrees or 2π radians, so the number 30 means a thirty-degree angle in DEG and a much larger angle of about 1,719 degrees in RAD. sin 30 returns 0.5 in DEG and -0.988031624093 in RAD. Set the mode before you press a trig key, and check it first whenever an answer looks wrong.

How do I type a power like 2 to the 10th?

Press 2, then the xʸ key, then 10, then equals, which gives 1024. The x² and x³ keys are shortcuts for the two most common cases and go after the number instead: press 7 then x² for 49. On a keyboard, the caret key does the same job as xʸ.

What does the EXP key do?

EXP enters the ×10ⁿ part of a number in scientific notation, so 6.02 EXP 23 means 6.02 × 10²³. It is a single key rather than typing out the multiplication and the power, and it is the reason 0.00000000015 can be entered as 1.5 EXP ± 10 in four presses.

How do the memory keys work?

M+ adds whatever is currently on screen to a stored number, and M− subtracts from it. MR pastes that stored number back into your expression, and once you have recalled it the same key reads MC for one press so you can wipe the store. A small M appears next to the mode when memory holds anything other than zero.

Can I use my keyboard instead of clicking?

Yes. Digits, a decimal point, plus, minus, asterisk, slash, caret and brackets all type straight in. Enter or the equals key evaluates, Backspace deletes the last entry, Escape clears everything, and p and t insert π and e. The full list is in the keyboard card under the calculator.

This calculator is provided for education and general reference. Results are computed in standard double-precision floating point and are accurate to roughly twelve significant digits, which is ample for schoolwork and most professional use but is not a substitute for an arbitrary-precision or symbolic system where exactness is required.

How this calculator works

There are two tools here because ratio questions come in two shapes. The first tab completes a proportion: give it any three of the four numbers in A:B = C:D and it supplies the one you left out. The second tab takes a single ratio and multiplies or divides both sides by a factor you choose.

Everything else on the screen is derived from those numbers rather than asked for separately — the simplified form, the two normalized readings, the percentage split and the shaded bar all come from the same pair.

A : B = C : D  →  A × D = B × C
missing D  =  (B × C) ÷ A
enlarged  =  A × n  :  B × n    shrunk  =  A ÷ n  :  B ÷ n

The first line is the one worth memorizing. In any true proportion the two diagonal products match, and that single fact is what every missing-value question reduces to, whichever box is empty.

What a ratio actually says

A ratio compares two quantities by division rather than by subtraction. Saying a mix is 3:4 cement to sand says nothing about how much you are making — it says that for every three units of cement there are four of sand. Three buckets and four buckets satisfy it; so do thirty and forty, and 0.75 of a bucket against one.

That is why a ratio has infinitely many equivalent forms and why the simplified version matters: it is the one form everybody can agree on. The order matters as much as the numbers. 3:4 cement to sand and 4:3 cement to sand describe two different mixes, and swapping the terms by accident is the single most common mistake in this topic.

Solving for the missing value

Cross multiplication turns a proportion into a one-step division. Suppose a recipe uses 3 parts flour to 4 parts water and you have committed to 600 g of flour. The proportion is 3:4 = 600:D, the diagonal products must match, and D falls out immediately:

3 × D = 4 × 600
D = 2400 ÷ 3 = 800

The same move works from any position. Leave A blank instead and the calculator divides the other way round. There is no need to rearrange anything by hand, but it is worth knowing which two numbers are being multiplied, because that is how you check an answer that looks wrong.

When the division does not come out evenly, two lines appear: the exact decimal and a rounded whole number underneath. Scaling 7:13 so the left side reads 1000 gives 1857.1428571429, and 1857 is the figure to use when you are counting physical objects rather than measuring a quantity.

Simplifying to lowest terms

Dividing both sides by their greatest common factor gives the smallest whole-number form. 18:24 becomes 3:4 by dividing both by 6. If the two sides share nothing, the ratio is already in lowest terms, which is why 7:13 stays as it is however long you stare at it.

The calculator also prints two normalized readings that are useful for comparison rather than for tidiness. Writing a ratio as 1:something answers "how much of the second for one of the first", and writing it as something:1 answers the reverse. For 3:4 those readings are 1:1.3333333333333 and 0.75:1. Neither is simpler to look at, but when you need to rank several ratios against each other they are the only forms that compare directly. Our GCF calculator will show the factor being divided out if you want to see that step on its own.

Enlarging and shrinking

Scaling keeps the relationship and changes the size. Multiply both sides by the same factor to enlarge, divide both by it to shrink. A 250:280 image enlarged 2.5 times becomes 625:700; shrunk by the same factor it becomes 100:112. In every case the simplified form is unchanged, which is exactly what makes it a scale rather than a distortion.

The practical catch is that scaling one side only is not scaling at all. Stretching a 1920:1080 image to 1920:1200 changes the shape of everything in it, which is the everyday version of getting a proportion wrong.

Ratios, fractions and percentages

The three forms describe the same split and trip people up because they count different things. A ratio compares part to part. A fraction and a percentage compare part to whole. Converting means adding the parts together first:

3 : 4  →  total parts = 3 + 4 = 7
first share  =  3 ÷ 7  =  42.857142857143%
second share  =  4 ÷ 7  =  57.142857142857%

Forgetting to add the parts is what produces the familiar error of calling 3:4 "75 percent". It is 75 percent of the second quantity, but only about 42.9 percent of the total, and which one is meant changes the answer entirely when splitting money or materials.

Where ratios show up

Screens and photographs are the most visible case: 16:9, 4:3 and 3:2 are all ratio statements, and a 1920×1080 display is simply 16:9 enlarged 120 times. Maps work the same way, a 1:25,000 scale meaning one unit on paper for 25,000 on the ground.

Away from pictures, ratios run kitchens and building sites — rice to water, cement to sand, paint tint to base — because the quantity changes constantly while the relationship must not. In business they describe gearing, current assets to current liabilities, staff to customers. In all of these the question is nearly always the same one the first tab answers: I have changed one side, what does the other side have to be?

Edge cases and common mistakes

  • Reversing the terms. 3:4 and 4:3 are different mixes. Write down which quantity is which before typing anything.
  • Mixing units. Both sides must be measured the same way. Comparing grams against cups produces a number with no meaning.
  • Treating the first number as the share of the whole. In 3:4 the first part is three sevenths of everything, not three quarters.
  • Zero. A ratio with a zero in it has no usable relationship and is refused here, as it is by the standard references.
  • Rounding too early. Round the final figure, not the intermediate one. Rounding 1857.1428571429 to 1857 before scaling again compounds the error.

What this page does not do

It works on two-term ratios. Three-part ratios such as 2:3:5 have to be taken a pair at a time, and ratios written with units attached need converting to bare numbers first. It does not convert between measurement systems, and it does not reduce the fractions it produces — our fraction calculator covers that, while the percentage calculator handles part-to-whole questions directly.

Every figure is worked out on your own device, and nothing you enter is transmitted or stored.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find the missing number in a ratio?

Enter the three values you have and leave the fourth box empty. The calculator cross multiplies: in A:B = C:D the two products A×D and B×C are always equal, so whichever box is blank can be worked out by multiplying the two values diagonally opposite it and dividing by the third.

What does it mean to simplify a ratio?

It means writing the same relationship with the smallest whole numbers possible. 18:24 simplifies to 3:4 because both sides divide by 6, their greatest common factor. The relationship is unchanged — three parts to four parts either way.

Is a ratio the same as a fraction?

They are written from the same numbers but they answer different questions. 3:4 compares one part to another part, so there are seven parts in total. The fraction 3/4 compares a part to the whole. Expressed as a fraction of everything, 3:4 is 3/7, which is about 42.9 percent.

How do I scale a ratio up or down?

Use the scaling tab. Enlarging by 2.5 multiplies both sides, so 250:280 becomes 625:700. Shrinking by 2.5 divides both sides, giving 100:112. Both sides must move by the same factor or the relationship changes.

Can I use decimals or negative numbers?

Decimals are fine and are handled exactly as typed. Negatives are accepted because the arithmetic still works, but a negative ratio rarely means anything in a real measurement, so check the sign if one appears unexpectedly.

Why does my answer come out as a long decimal?

Because the numbers do not divide evenly. 7:13 scaled so the left side is 1000 gives 1857.1428571429 on the right. The rounded whole number is shown beneath it, which is the figure you want for anything counted in whole units.

Does this handle three-part ratios like 2:3:5?

Not directly — this page works on two-term ratios. A three-part ratio can be handled one pair at a time, keeping the shared term fixed, or simplified by dividing all three parts by their greatest common factor.

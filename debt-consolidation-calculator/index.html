How This Calculator Works

Add every debt you're considering rolling into a new loan — its name, current balance, monthly payment, and interest rate. The calculator combines them into a single weighted-average rate (larger balances count more toward the average, since they cost more in interest) and estimates how long you'd take to become debt-free and how much interest you'd pay if you kept making those same separate payments. Then enter the terms of the consolidation loan you're being offered — amount, rate, term, and any upfront fee — and the calculator builds the same payoff-time and total-interest picture for that single new loan. The verdict banner at the top compares the two total costs directly, in dollars, so you're not left guessing.

The Math Behind the Comparison

For your current debts, the calculator treats the combined balance as if it were one loan being paid down at your combined monthly payment and a weighted-average interest rate — the same approach most bank and credit-union consolidation calculators use. From there it solves for how many months it would take to reach zero, and multiplies that by your monthly payment to find the total interest paid.

For the new loan, it uses the standard installment-loan payment formula to find your fixed monthly payment from the amount, rate, and term, then works out the total interest over the life of the loan. Finally, it adds the upfront fee on top and solves — using an iterative rate search, the same underlying approach lenders use to disclose APR — for the real, fee-adjusted APR of the new loan. That real APR is usually a little higher than the advertised rate, because a $500 fee on a $12,500 loan means you're effectively only receiving $12,000 in usable funds while repaying the full $12,500 plus interest.

What Debts to Include (and Leave Out)

Debt consolidation calculators work best with unsecured, higher-rate revolving or installment debt — credit cards, store cards, medical bills, and existing personal loans are the classic candidates. These typically carry rates well above what a consolidation loan can offer, so rolling them together tends to produce real savings.

Leave secured debts like a mortgage or auto loan out of this calculator, along with low-rate federal student loans. Those usually have purpose-built, better options — a mortgage or auto refinance, or a federal student loan consolidation/income-driven repayment program — rather than being folded into a general personal-loan consolidation, where you could lose favorable terms or protections specific to that debt type.

When Consolidation Actually Saves You Money

Consolidation pays off when the new loan's real APR — after accounting for any fee — comes in meaningfully below the weighted-average rate you're currently paying across your debts. The bigger that gap, and the higher your current balances, the more you stand to save in total interest. It also helps when your current payments barely cover the interest on high-rate cards, since a fixed-term installment loan guarantees an actual payoff date, where minimum credit card payments alone often don't.

It's less clearly worth it when the rate gap is small, when the loan fee eats up most of the rate advantage, or when stretching to a much longer term lowers your monthly payment but increases total interest paid. Always compare the total interest + fees figure, not just the monthly payment — a lower payment can hide a more expensive loan overall.

The Hidden Cost: Loan Fees and Real APR

Lenders often quote an attractive interest rate while charging an origination fee or "points" — a percentage of the loan taken out upfront, either deducted from your proceeds or added to the balance. A 3–5% fee is common on personal and consolidation loans. That fee doesn't show up in the advertised rate, but it absolutely affects what the loan really costs you, which is exactly what the real APR figure in this calculator captures.

As a rule of thumb: the shorter the loan term, the more a flat fee distorts the real APR, since it's spread over fewer months of payments. Always compare loan offers using real APR — not the headline rate — when deciding between lenders.

Consolidation vs. Debt Avalanche vs. Debt Snowball

Debt consolidation replaces your existing debts with one new loan. The debt avalanche and debt snowball methods, by contrast, are repayment strategies for the debts you already have — no new borrowing involved. Avalanche directs any extra payment at your highest-interest debt first, which minimizes total interest paid mathematically. Snowball directs extra payments at your smallest balance first, which pays off individual debts faster and can build momentum and motivation, even though it usually costs a bit more in total interest.

These aren't mutually exclusive with consolidation — you can consolidate the high-rate debts that benefit most from a lower rate, and use avalanche or snowball on whatever's left. If you'd rather explore that route without taking on a new loan, our Credit Card Payoff Calculator can help you plan it out.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most common mistake is comparing only the advertised interest rate and ignoring the upfront fee — a lower headline rate with a large fee can end up costing more than a slightly higher no-fee rate. A second is choosing the longest available term purely to shrink the monthly payment, without checking what that does to total interest. A third, and arguably the most damaging long-term, is consolidating credit card debt and then running the old cards back up — you end up with the original balances plus the new consolidation loan, doubling your total debt instead of reducing it. Finally, people sometimes include secured or low-rate debt (a car loan, a subsidized student loan) in the mix, when those debts usually have better, purpose-specific alternatives than a general consolidation loan.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if debt consolidation is worth it?

Compare the total interest plus fees you'd pay on the consolidation loan against the total interest you'd pay sticking with your current debts. If the consolidation loan's total cost is lower — and ideally your monthly payment drops too — it's usually worth it. This calculator does that comparison for you automatically, including the upfront loan fee most people forget to factor in.

What is the "real APR" and why does it matter?

The real APR is the interest rate the loan effectively costs once you account for upfront fees or points, not just the advertised rate. Because a loan fee reduces how much cash you actually receive while you still repay the full amount, the true cost of borrowing is always a little higher than the sticker rate — sometimes enough to erase the savings you expected.

Which debts should I include in the calculator?

Include unsecured, higher-rate debts you're actually planning to roll into the new loan — typically credit cards, store cards, and existing personal loans. Leave out secured debts like a mortgage or auto loan and low-rate federal student loans, since those usually have better options (refinancing) than consolidation.

Does debt consolidation lower my total debt?

No — consolidation restructures debt, it doesn't erase it. You still owe the same principal (plus whatever interest and fees the new loan carries). What changes is how you pay it back: one loan, one monthly payment, and — if you qualify for a lower rate — potentially less interest over time.

Will debt consolidation hurt my credit score?

Applying triggers a hard inquiry, which can cause a small, temporary dip. Beyond that, paying off revolving credit card balances with an installment loan often improves your credit utilization ratio, which can help your score over the following months — as long as you make every new payment on time and avoid running the old cards back up.

What's the difference between debt consolidation and the debt avalanche or snowball method?

Avalanche and snowball are repayment strategies for the debts you already have — avalanche pays the highest-rate debt first, snowball pays the smallest balance first — without taking on any new loan. Debt consolidation replaces those separate debts with a single new loan. You can use either strategy on your existing debts if you decide not to consolidate.

What if my calculated payoff time shows "Never"?

That means your current monthly payment on that debt is too low to even cover the interest charged each month — the balance would grow forever rather than shrink. This is a strong signal that either a lower-rate consolidation loan or a larger payment is genuinely needed, not just a nice-to-have.

Should I choose the longest loan term to get the lowest monthly payment?

Not automatically. A longer term lowers your monthly payment but usually increases the total interest paid over the life of the loan, since you're borrowing the same money for more months. Try a few different term lengths in the calculator and compare the total interest column, not just the monthly payment, before deciding.

What VAT is, and who actually pays it

Value-added tax is charged at every stage of a supply chain, but only once in economic terms. A business adds VAT to what it sells, reclaims the VAT on what it buys, and hands the difference to the tax authority. Do that at each link in the chain and the total collected comes to exactly the rate applied to the final retail price — the tax lands on the end consumer, while every business in between is really acting as an unpaid collector.

That design is why VAT is so widely used. More than 170 countries operate one, and it accounts for a large share of government revenue almost everywhere it exists. It is also why VAT resists the evasion that a single-stage tax invites: because each business reclaims what its supplier charged, both sides have a paper trail and an incentive to declare the transaction.

For anyone using a calculator, the practical consequence is that two different prices exist for the same thing. The net price excludes VAT and is what a VAT-registered buyer effectively pays, since they reclaim the tax. The gross price includes it and is what a consumer pays. Business-to-business quotes are usually net; retail prices in the UK and the EU must be shown gross. Confusing the two is the single most common source of a mispriced quote.

How this calculator works

All of it rests on one relationship. VAT is a percentage of the net price, and the gross price is the two added together:

gross = net + VAT
VAT = net × rate ÷ 100

Rearranging that gives everything else. The three tabs are three ways into the same equation:

  • Add VAT — you have a net price and want the total. gross = net × (1 + rate ÷ 100). At 20%, £480 × 1.20 = £576, of which £96 is VAT.
  • Remove VAT — you have a gross total from a receipt or invoice and want the figure underneath it. net = gross ÷ (1 + rate ÷ 100). At 20%, £2,340 ÷ 1.20 = £1,950, so the VAT was £390.
  • Any two values — fill in any two of rate, net, gross and VAT amount and the other two follow. This covers the cases the first two tabs do not: working out what rate was applied when you have both prices, or recovering the prices when all you know is the rate and the VAT charged.

The rate itself can be derived whenever you have two amounts:

rate = (gross − net) ÷ net × 100

A £480 net price and a £576 total gives (576 − 480) ÷ 480 × 100 = 20%. This is genuinely useful when a supplier sends a total with no breakdown, or when you are checking whether a foreign invoice used the rate you expected.

One consequence surprises people: VAT is never the same percentage of the gross price as the rate itself. At a 20% rate, the VAT is 20% of the net but only 16.67% of the total. That gap is the whole reason the reverse calculation needs a division rather than a subtraction.

The mistake that makes reverse VAT wrong

Take £2,340 including VAT at 20% and subtract 20% of it. You get £1,872. It looks like the answer and it is not: the correct net price is £1,950, and the two differ by £78.

The reason is that the 20% was applied to £1,950, not to £2,340. Twenty per cent of the smaller figure is £390; twenty per cent of the larger one is £468. Subtracting the wrong one leaves you short. The error scales with the amount, so it stays invisible on a coffee receipt and becomes expensive on a £40,000 invoice.

There are two reliable ways to avoid it. Divide by 1.20 rather than subtracting, or use the VAT fraction: at 20% the tax inside any gross figure is exactly one sixth of it, so £2,340 ÷ 6 = £390 of VAT, leaving £1,950. At the 5% reduced rate the fraction is one twenty-first. The calculator shows the applicable fraction under each result so you can sanity-check the arithmetic without trusting the tool blindly.

Standard, reduced, zero — and exempt

Most VAT systems run several rates at once. The UK's structure is typical: a 20% standard rate on the great majority of goods and services, a 5% reduced rate on a short list including domestic fuel and power and children's car seats, and a 0% rate on most food, children's clothing, books and newspapers. Which rate applies depends on what is sold, not on who sells it — the same shop can be charging all three in a single transaction.

The distinction worth understanding is between zero-rated and exempt, because they look identical to the customer and are not at all the same for the seller. A zero-rated supply sits inside the VAT system at a rate of 0%: no VAT is charged, but the business still reclaims the VAT on its own costs. An exempt supply — insurance, most education, health services, residential rent — sits outside the system: no VAT is charged and the related input VAT generally cannot be reclaimed, so the tax becomes a cost the business absorbs.

The practical upshot is that a business making only exempt supplies usually cannot register for VAT at all, while one making zero-rated supplies often registers voluntarily and ends up reclaiming more than it charges.

VAT around the world, and why GST is the same thing

Standard rates vary far more than people expect. Within the EU they run from 17% in Luxembourg to 27% in Hungary, against an average of roughly 22% and a legal floor of 15% that member states may not go below. Switzerland, outside the EU, charges 8.1% — the lowest in western Europe by a wide margin, and a well-known driver of cross-border shopping from France, Germany and Austria.

Outside Europe the same tax usually answers to a different name. Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Singapore and India all call theirs a goods and services tax, and the mechanism is identical: charged at each stage, reclaimed by businesses, borne by the final consumer. The rates tend to be lower — 10% in Australia, 15% in New Zealand, 5% for Canada's federal GST — and the exception lists differ enough that the local rules matter far more than the label. No country operates a VAT and a GST as two separate taxes.

The rate table beside the calculator covers the jurisdictions people ask about most often, and the country selector loads the right standard rate straight into the form. Rates move with national budgets more often than you would think, so treat any table — including this one — as a starting point rather than the last word before you file a return.

VAT compared with US sales tax

The United States is the only large economy without a national VAT. It uses retail sales tax instead, set by states and often by counties and cities on top, and collected once at the final sale rather than at every stage.

Three differences matter in practice. Sales tax rates are much lower — typically 4% to 10% against 15% to 27% for VAT — because a single-stage tax has to be collected in one hit rather than accumulated. Sales tax is added at the till and quoted separately, so the shelf price is the net price, whereas UK and EU retail prices must be shown VAT-inclusive. And sales tax can cascade: when a business buys something taxed and resells it without a valid exemption certificate, the tax is charged twice on the same value, which VAT's reclaim mechanism prevents by design.

Neither system is obviously fairer. VAT is regressive in its raw form, taking a larger share of a low income than a high one, which is why most VAT countries zero-rate or reduce the rate on food, children's clothing and domestic energy. Sales tax has the same problem and generally the same partial remedy. If you need US figures rather than VAT, the sales tax calculator handles state and local rates directly.

Invoices, rounding and registration

A compliant VAT invoice needs more than a total. It generally has to show the supplier's VAT number, the rate applied, the net amount, the VAT amount and the gross total as separate figures — which is exactly the breakdown the result panel produces, so it can be copied straight across.

Rounding causes more confusion than it deserves. Tax authorities generally permit VAT to be calculated on the invoice total rather than line by line, and their rounding conventions differ: some allow rounding down to the nearest penny, others require ordinary rounding. A line-by-line total and an invoice-level total can therefore differ by a penny or two on a long document. That is normal and accepted, not an error to chase.

Registration is a separate question from calculation. Every country sets a turnover threshold above which registering becomes compulsory — the UK's has been £90,000 of taxable turnover in any rolling twelve-month period since 2024, while some EU states set it at zero for non-resident sellers. Voluntary registration below the threshold can still pay off if your customers are themselves VAT-registered and reclaim what you charge them, since it lets you reclaim your own input VAT while costing your customers nothing.

What this calculator does not cover

A rate-and-amount tool answers one question well and deliberately leaves the harder ones alone.

  • Which rate applies to your product. Classification is where most VAT disputes actually happen, and it turns on detail no calculator can see. The tool applies whatever rate you give it.
  • Place-of-supply rules. For cross-border and digital sales the country whose VAT applies is often the customer's, not yours, and schemes such as the EU's One Stop Shop change how it is reported rather than which rate is due.
  • Flat rate and margin schemes. Simplified schemes charge a fixed percentage of gross turnover or tax only the margin on second-hand goods. Neither follows the standard arithmetic here.
  • Reverse charge. On many B2B cross-border supplies the buyer accounts for the VAT rather than the seller, so the invoice shows no VAT at all.
  • Exemptions and partial exemption. Whether input VAT is reclaimable, and in what proportion, is a separate calculation from the tax on a sale.

For anything you will actually file, check the rate and the treatment with the relevant tax authority or an accountant.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remove VAT from a price?

Divide the gross price by 1 plus the rate as a decimal. At the UK standard rate of 20% that means dividing by 1.20: a gross total of £2,340 becomes £1,950 net, with £390 of VAT. Do not subtract 20% from the gross figure — that gives £1,872, which is £78 short, because the 20% was charged on the smaller net price rather than on the total.

How do I add VAT to a price?

Multiply the net price by 1 plus the rate as a decimal. At 20%, multiply by 1.20 — a net price of £480 becomes £576 gross, with £96 of VAT. At the reduced 5% rate you multiply by 1.05 instead, so £850 net becomes £892.50.

What is the VAT rate in the UK?

The standard rate is 20% and has been since January 2011. A reduced rate of 5% applies to a limited list including domestic fuel and power, children's car seats and some energy-saving materials, and a zero rate applies to most food, children's clothing, books and newspapers. Which rate applies depends on what is being sold, not on who is selling it.

What is the quick way to work out VAT in my head?

Use the VAT fraction. At 20% the VAT contained in a gross price is exactly one sixth of it, so a £2,340 total divides by 6 to give £390 of VAT. At the 5% rate the fraction is one twenty-first. This is the same arithmetic as dividing by 1.20, just easier to do without a calculator.

What is the difference between zero-rated and exempt?

Zero-rated supplies are inside the VAT system but charged at 0%, so the business still reclaims the VAT it paid on its own costs. Exempt supplies are outside the system altogether: no VAT is charged and the related input VAT generally cannot be reclaimed. The customer pays nothing either way, but the effect on the seller's costs is very different.

Is VAT the same as GST?

Mechanically, yes. Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Singapore and India call their value-added tax a goods and services tax, and it works the same way — charged at each stage, reclaimed by businesses, borne by the final consumer. The rates and the lists of exceptions differ a great deal, so the name is worth less than the local rules. No country runs a VAT and a GST side by side.

Why does the US not have VAT?

It is the only large economy without one. American states levy a retail sales tax instead, collected once at the final sale rather than at every stage of the supply chain. A US business selling to customers in the UK or EU may still have to register for VAT there, which is the usual reason an American visitor ends up on a page like this one.

Does VAT get rounded per item or per invoice?

Tax authorities generally let you calculate VAT on the invoice total rather than line by line, and rounding conventions vary between them — some allow rounding down to the nearest penny or cent, others require normal rounding. Line-by-line and total-based calculations can differ by a penny or two on a long invoice, which is normal rather than an error. This calculator rounds the final figures to two decimal places.
